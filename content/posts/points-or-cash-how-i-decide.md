Having enough points for a flight does not automatically mean I should use them.

Sometimes a $180 ticket is a terrible place to spend 30,000 valuable points. Other times an expensive last-minute flight can be exactly where points shine.

I like making the decision with a simple comparison rather than relying on a universal cents-per-point target.

## Start with the real cash price

Look at the ticket or hotel you would actually buy.

Do not compare your points redemption with a $4,000 business-class ticket if you would never pay $4,000 for it. That can be fun for showing off a theoretical redemption value, but it is not always useful for personal decision-making.

Use the real alternative.

## Subtract the cash required on the award

Awards often still have taxes and fees.

If the cash ticket is $800 and the award is 50,000 points plus $100, the points are not replacing $800. They are replacing $700.

That distinction matters.

## Account for rewards you would earn on the paid booking

Paying cash can generate airline miles, hotel points, elite credit, or credit-card rewards.

When the decision is close, I factor that in.

If a paid hotel stay would earn a meaningful number of points and help with status, the effective cost of paying cash is a little lower than the sticker price.

## Think about what else the points could do

This is the opportunity-cost question.

If using 80,000 points saves $600 today but I am planning another trip where those same points could save $1,500, I may pay cash for the first trip and save the points.

That only works if the second trip is realistic. I do not hoard points forever waiting for a mythical perfect redemption.

## Use points when cash prices are painful

Points can be especially useful when cash prices spike.

That might happen during:

- Holidays
- Major events
- Last-minute travel
- One-way international trips
- Premium cabins
- Expensive city-center hotels

If the award price has not increased as much as the cash price, the redemption can become much more attractive.

## Use cash when the fare is already cheap

Cheap economy fares are often where I am most willing to pay cash.

If a flight is inexpensive and the award price is relatively high, preserving the points can make sense.

The same applies to hotels. A $110 independent hotel can be a better choice than using a large chunk of hotel points just because an award is available nearby.

## Do not forget flexibility

Cancellation rules can tilt the decision.

Some award bookings are easier or cheaper to cancel than certain cash fares. Other programs may charge fees or have restrictive rules.

If my plans are uncertain, flexibility has real value even if it is difficult to assign a perfect dollar amount.

## A simple value calculation

When I want a quick benchmark, I use:

**Cash price minus award taxes and fees, divided by points required.**

Then multiply by 100 to express the result in cents per point.

For example, if the cash ticket is $700 and the award is 40,000 points plus $100:

The points are replacing $600 of cash value. $600 divided by 40,000 equals 1.5 cents per point.

That is not a rule telling me whether to book. It is simply a way to compare this redemption with my other options.

## My decision rule

I usually ask four questions:

- Would I actually pay the cash price?
- Is the redemption replacing a meaningful amount of cash?
- Do I have a better near-term use for the points?
- Does one option give me much better flexibility?

If the points let me take a trip I would otherwise skip because the cash cost is too high, that can be more important than squeezing out the mathematically perfect redemption.

Points are a tool. The best redemption is ultimately the one that helps you take the trip you want without creating a financial problem somewhere else.
